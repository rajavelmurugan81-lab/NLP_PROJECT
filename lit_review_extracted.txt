2024
Zero-shot Scientific Claim Verification Using LLMs and Citation Text – Alvarez et al.
GPT-3.5/GPT-4, zero-shot & few-shot prompting, SCitance dataset
Shows that LLMs can perform scientific claim verification without expensive supervised fine-tuning. GPT-4 achieved competitive performance with supervised approaches. 
Depends on citation-text/abstract evidence and prompting. It does not provide persistent memory of verification failures or adaptive verification for repeated errors.
2
2024
Retrieval Augmented Scientific Claim Verification – Liu et al.
RAG + BM25/Lucene + ColBERT/FAISS + T5 reranking + BERT ensemble + PICO
CliVER retrieves clinical-trial evidence and classifies claims as Support, Refute, Neutral. It achieved F1 = 0.92 on CoVERt. 
Mainly biomedical/clinical. Retrieval and sentence-selection errors can affect final verification. It does not learn structured patterns from previous failures.
3
2024/2025
A Survey of Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) for Large Language Models
Naive RAG, Advanced RAG, Modular RAG; sparse, dense and hybrid retrieval
Provides a systematic view of RAG evolution, including query rewriting, query expansion, decomposition, reranking and agentic RAG. 
RAG can still suffer from irrelevant retrieval, poor retriever-generator alignment and context conflicts. Adaptive learning based on previous interactions/failures remains an important direction.
4
2025
A Survey on Hallucination in Large Language Models: Principles, Taxonomy, Challenges, and Open Questions – Huang et al.
Hallucination taxonomy + detection + mitigation + RAG analysis
Gives a comprehensive taxonomy and identifies retrieval failure and generation bottlenecks as important causes of RAG hallucinations. 
Survey identifies the need for adaptive RAG and better handling of irrelevant retrieval, but does not implement a persistent failure-learning mechanism.
5
2025
Hallucination Detection in LLMs via Beam Search Sampling and Semantic Consistency Analysis – Bouchekir et al.
Beam Search Sampling + semantic clustering + NLI + CoVe
Generates multiple candidate answers and checks semantic consistency. Reports precision 0.87 and AUROC 0.81 for multi-answer generation. 
Multiple generations increase computation/latency. It focuses on consistency of current outputs rather than remembering and learning from historical failure patterns.
6
2025
Towards Detecting LLMs Hallucination via Markov Chain-based Multi-agent Debate Framework – Sun et al.
Claim detection + evidence retrieval + Trust/Skeptic/Leader agents + Markov Chain
Introduces multi-agent debate for hallucination detection and improves verification compared with baselines. 
Agents debate the current evidence but do not maintain a structured failure history that changes future verification.
7
2025
Retrieval Augmented Generation based Explainable Fact-Checking using Knowledge Graphs
RAG + Knowledge Graph + TransE, TransH, ConvE, RotatE, DistMult
Uses structured graph evidence for explainable fact checking and reports 14% accuracy and 8% macro-F1 improvement over the Llama-2 7B baseline. 
KG-based reasoning is promising but the work does not integrate historical failure patterns, adaptive verification or failure-aware memory into the RAG pipeline.
8
2025
SciClaimHunt: A Large Dataset for Evidence-based Scientific Claim Verification
LLM-based claim generation + positive/negative claims + section-wise evidence + multi-head attention
Introduces large-scale scientific claim datasets, including SciClaimHunt Num for numerical/cardinal claims. The CEM method retrieves evidence from different paper sections and uses multi-head attention for claim-evidence matching. 
Existing scientific claim datasets have limited size and often rely on abstracts. Dataset-based approaches do not themselves provide an adaptive verification/memory mechanism.
9
2025
Valsci: An Open-source, Self-hostable Literature Review Utility for Automated Large-batch Scientific Claim Verification using LLMs – Edelman & Skolnick
RAG + Semantic Scholar + query expansion + bibliometric scoring + CoT + asynchronous processing
Provides scalable, open-source, end-to-end scientific claim verification. It reduces citation hallucination and can process claims at high throughput. 
Semantic Scholar coverage can be incomplete and paywalled papers may be missing. Bibliometric scoring can introduce bias. It does not maintain structured failure signatures for future adaptive verification. 
10
2025
Mitigation of Hallucinations in Language Models in Education: A New Approach of Comparative and Cross-Verification
Comparative verification + cross-verification
Investigates hallucination mitigation by comparing/cross-checking outputs from language models in an educational context. 
Cross-verification can identify inconsistencies, but the approach does not establish a persistent failure-learning loop that recognizes the same reasoning error in future tasks.
11
2026
Empirical Evaluation of Self and Cross-Model Verification for LLM Hallucination Detection in Cybersecurity
Self-verification + cross-model verification + human ground truth
Evaluates five LLMs on cybersecurity tasks. Cross-verification detected hallucinations at 56%, compared with 36.4% for self-verification. It also found that verification was poorly calibrated. 
Cross-verification performs better but produces more false alarms and remains poorly calibrated. It does not use historical failures to determine when stronger verification is needed.
12
2026
SciTrue: Evidence-Grounded Claim Verification in Science – Tan et al.
Claim refinement + literature retrieval + evidence extraction + subclaim decomposition + source-level attribution
Provides fine-grained attribution by linking claim components to explicit scientific evidence and emphasizes transparent auditability. 
Depends on Semantic Scholar coverage and metadata. It improves provenance and attribution but does not provide a Hallucination Ledger + Failure Signature + historical failure matching mechanism. 
13
2026
VISTA: Verification in Sequential Turn-based Assessment
Atomic claim decomposition + source verification + dialogue-history verification + sequential memory
Models factuality as a dynamic property across conversations. Verified claims are stored in a dynamic knowledge store for subsequent turns. 
Its memory is mainly for maintaining dialogue factual consistency. It does not specifically store classified hallucination/failure signatures and use them to trigger different verification strategies.
14
2026
Hallucinations in Generative Artificial Intelligence and Large Language Models: Tests, Datasets, Detection and Correction Methods – Naser
Hallucination taxonomy + detection + correction + RAG + uncertainty + external verification
Provides a broad review of hallucination causes, detection methods, datasets, correction techniques and evaluation metrics. It highlights retrieval failures, outdated knowledge and contextual problems. 
Mainly a comprehensive review rather than an adaptive implementation. It highlights the need for better detection/correction but does not provide a closed-loop mechanism where detected failures are stored and reused to improve future verification.

3. Research Gaps Found From All 14 Papers
Gap 1 — No structured failure memory
Most existing approaches detect whether a current claim is wrong.
They answer:
"Is this claim hallucinated?"
But they generally don't answer:
"Have I made this type of mistake before?"
Your CiteGuard stores the failure and its structural characteristics in a Hallucination Ledger. 

Gap 2 — No Failure Signature
A normal memory system might store:
"Treatment X works for all patients" → Wrong.
Your system stores why it was wrong:
Failure Type: Overgeneralization
Claim Scope: Broad
Evidence Scope: Narrow
Population: Mismatch
Evidence Type: Clinical Study
This is the Failure Signature.
The project design specifically defines failure types such as unsupported claim, citation mismatch, overgeneralization, causal leap, outdated evidence, population/context mismatch, contradictory evidence and evidence misinterpretation.

5. Gap 4 — Verification Is Not Fully Adaptive
The RAG survey itself identifies adaptive retrieval and learning from previous interactions as an important future direction. 
Similarly, the 2026 cybersecurity study shows that verification is not properly calibrated: actual hallucination rates can vary substantially while verifier flag rates remain relatively similar.

SciTrue specifically demonstrates the importance of mapping claims to explicit source evidence because normal LLM/RAG systems can provide citations that do not actually support the generated statement.

7. Gap 6 — Retrieval Itself Can Cause Hallucination
The Huang survey identifies retrieval failure and generation bottleneck as major sources of hallucination in RAG systems. 
Examples include:
wrong document retrieved 
irrelevant evidence 
ambiguous query 
incomplete evidence 
outdated evidence 
poor embedding 
noisy context 
Therefore, simply adding RAG does not guarantee factuality.
CiteGuard improvement
The Skeptic/Verifier should check:
Did we retrieve the right paper?
Does the exact evidence support the claim?
Is the evidence current?
Is the claim broader than the evidence?

10. Final Research Gap — Strongest Version
You can use this directly in your PPT / Review document:
Although existing research has made significant progress in RAG-based scientific claim verification, hallucination detection, source attribution, multi-agent verification, and cross-model verification, most systems focus on detecting errors in the current interaction. Existing approaches do not sufficiently address how detected failures can be structurally represented, remembered, matched with future claim-evidence situations, and used to dynamically strengthen verification. Recent studies also identify retrieval failures, irrelevant evidence, poor attribution, and verification calibration as continuing challenges. Therefore, there is a need for a failure-aware adaptive verification framework that combines claim-level provenance, hallucination classification, structured failure signatures, historical failure matching, and adaptive verification in a closed feedback loop.
This is strongly aligned with your CiteGuard design.
